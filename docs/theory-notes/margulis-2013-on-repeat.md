## Findings that bear on generation
1. **Repetition itself raises liking.** In Margulis (2013) listeners heard original
   excerpts of 20th-century art music, versions with *immediate* repetition inserted,
   and versions with *delayed* repetition inserted (segments repeated later). The
   repetition versions were rated as more enjoyable, interesting and artful, even
   though the insertions were made mechanically, without regard to musical
   principle.
2. **Speech-to-song illusion** (Deutsch et al.): about 85% of listeners hear a
   repeated spoken phrase turn into song after enough exact repetitions. Exact
   repetition is a musicalising operation.
3. **Wundt curve (inverted U) for repeated exposure.** Liking rises with familiarity, then
   falls with satiation. Two explanations are offered: perceptual fluency (we
   misattribute processing ease to the music, until we notice) and Berlyne's two
   opposing drives (a preference for the familiar, then an urge for novelty).
4. **Satiation timescale scales with complexity.** Szpunar, Schellenberg & Pliner
   (2004): with 15-second excerpts, richer, more complex, real music gained more in
   liking at first, and still eventually satiated. Tan et al. (2006): repeated hearings
   *increased* liking for patchwork pieces (sections reordered) but not intact ones.
5. **Margulis's own reading:** familiarity rewards because it lets the listener
   attend at different levels (timbre, inner voices, large-scale structure) on
   successive passes. Simple stimuli leave too little to discover, so they satiate
   faster.
6. **Musematic vs discursive repetition** (Middleton): immediate repetition of short
   elements (loops, riffs; the listener can choose where to attend, "an
   ever-changing same") versus larger-scale section returns (rondos, binary forms).
   They behave differently; the temporal gap between recurrences changes how they work.
7. **Debussy** repeats 60–70% of phrases (a cited estimate); heavy repetition is
   normal in art music, not just pop.
8. **Earworms** are usually short looped melodic fragments (a few measures) rather
   than harmonic loops.

## What it means for us
1. **Don't fear repetition; vary what repeats.** The product *is* a long loop. The
   evidence says: keep an exact core (melodic cell, bass figure, groove) and let
   *other layers* change across passes (timbre, inner voice, register, ornament,
   dynamics, space), because repetition rewards attention at multiple levels.
2. **Two repetition timescales** map to our design: musematic (2-bar groove cell,
   riff, ostinato; keep exact for several statements) and discursive (the 4–8 bar
   phrase returning after a contrasting phrase or a breakdown; keep recognisable,
   vary the setting). Don't use the same variation policy for both.
3. **A satiation budget.** For a simple preset (few layers, few notes) rotate or evolve
   sooner; for a rich preset allow longer repetition. This is the Wundt curve in code:
   time since the cell first appeared, number of exact repetitions, and number of
   active layers.
4. **Delayed repetition counts.** Bringing back a phrase after a different one raised
   ratings as much as immediate repetition did in her study. Returns after
   breakdowns are well supported.
5. **Loop-friendly material.** Early in a piece, repeat exact short cells ("the loop
   sets up"), later vary around them. This is the speech-to-song pattern and fits
   the existing intro/build/full form.
6. **Caveat.** Her stimuli are short art-music excerpts in lab sessions; the evidence for
   hour-long ambient listening is indirect. Treat the numbers as directional.
